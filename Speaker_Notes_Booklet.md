**Slide 28 -- Stacked-outcome methods**

Time slot: 29:25 -- 30:55, ~205 words

1. Here's the test in three steps.
2. Step one -- reshape.
3. We take each student's row and stretch it into five rows, one per substance.
4. Same student.
5. Same life experiences.
6. Just one row for opioids, one for alcohol, one for marijuana, one for cigarettes, one for cocaine.

7. Step two -- one regression.
8. The model has three kinds of terms.
9. First, the seven life experiences themselves -- bullying, suicidal thinking, using multiple substances, risky behavior, family struggles, sexual victimization, unsafe surroundings.
10. Second, the substance column from the reshaped table -- opioids, alcohol, marijuana, cigarettes, and cocaine -- so each substance gets its own baseline rate.
11. And third, interactions between every life experience and every substance -- like bullying-alcohol, or bullying-marijuana, and so on.
12. Each interaction asks one specific question: does this life experience's link change depending on which substance we're looking at?
13. With seven life experiences and four non-opioid substances, that's 28 interactions.

14. Step three -- one test on all 28 interactions at once.
15. If any of them are non-zero, life experiences do link differently to different substances.
16. The opioid-specific signal is real.
17. The individual interactions then tell us which experiences and how much.

**Slide 29 -- KHB decomposition**

Time slot: 30:55 -- 32:10, ~185 words

1. Step one -- the follow-up question.
2. The previous slide showed bullying links to opioids more than to other substances.
3. But that raises one more question: is the link really about opioids, or is it just that bullied teens use a lot of substances in general, and opioids come along for the ride?

4. Step two -- we split each link into two parts.
5. The direct part is bullying to opioids through some other path, most likely emotional pain.
6. The indirect part is bullying makes a teen use a lot of substances, and one of those happens to be opioids.
7. Total equals direct plus indirect.

8. Step three -- the answer for bullying.
9. About 10 percent of the link runs through "uses a lot." The other 90 percent is direct.
10. If most of the link had been indirect, the opioid-specific story would have collapsed.
11. It didn't.
12. The story holds.

13. One important caveat: this is an accounting split, not a causal proof.
14. With one snapshot in time, we can describe how much of the link runs each way.
15. We can't prove what causes what.

**Slide 34 -- Thesis landed**

1. Here's the claim, now backed by evidence.
2. Effective adolescent prevention is both integrated across co-occurring problems, and specific to what drives each of them.

3. Paper 1 showed that integration barely exists in practice -- only three rigorous trials worldwide.
4. And the trial that worked best wasn't the one that taught both topics.
5. Paper 2 showed how integration actually works when it does -- through slow norm change in schools, over years.

7. Prevention that's integrated but not specific wastes resources on what's shared.
8. Prevention that's specific but not integrated misses what crosses over.
9. The evidence asks for both.
